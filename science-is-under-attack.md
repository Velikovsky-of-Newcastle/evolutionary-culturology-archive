Historical vs. Modern Trends in Attacks on Science
Have attacks increased or decreased over time?
 The
nature of attacks has evolved:
Blatant
     Persecution vs. Subtle Undermining:
 Historically (17th–20th
     centuries), attacks often involved overt suppression – trials, book bans,
     imprisonments – usually by centralized authorities (church or state).
     Those still occur (e.g., Turkey’s 2016 academic purge, China’s treatment
     of dissident scientists), but in many parts of the world overt crackdowns
     are less common in the 21st century. Instead, 
modern attacks are often
     indirect – via disinformation, politicization of research funding, or
     regulatory interference rather than burning books
. For example, no
     equivalent of Galileo’s trial is happening in Western countries today;
     scientists are generally free to publish. However, the 
scale of
     disinformation now is unprecedented
, reaching billions through the
     internet. In that sense, attacks have 
increased in reach
, if not in
     formal severity.
Global
     Spread:
 In the past, attacks on science might be localized (one
     church, one regime). Today, misinformation respects no borders – a
     conspiracy theory seeded in one country can go viral worldwide. The COVID
     infodemic demonstrated simultaneous science denial on every continent. So
     contemporary attacks, especially via social media, are 
more globally
     synchronized
, which can magnify their impact.
Public
     Trust Resilience:
 Despite waves of denial, overall public trust in
     science remains relatively high (e.g., ~80% of Americans trusted
     scientists in 2020 at the start of COVID, though that fell to ~70% by 2021
     amid polarized messaging (
Public
     Trust in Scientists and Views on Their Role in Policymaking
) (
Public
     Perceptions of Science and Technology
)). 
Over centuries, education
     levels have risen and scientific literacy (in some countries) is better,
     potentially immunizing against certain anti-science claims. For instance,
     creationism has far less traction in Europe or East Asia than in the 1920s
     U.S. This suggests that in some regions, the Enlightenment values have
     deeply taken root. On the other hand, 
political polarization can erode
     that progress
 quickly (as seen in the U.S. partisan trust gap).
Institutionalization
     of Science:
 Modern science is embedded in universities, companies,
     international bodies (UN, IPCC, etc.). This institutional strength can
     buffer direct attacks (e.g., even if one government denies climate change,
     the international scientific community continues its assessments).
     Historically, science relied on patronage of kings or church – more
     vulnerable to singular decrees. Now, a nation cutting funding (like the
     U.S. under Trump) doesn’t stop other nations or private entities from
     advancing science (indeed, states, cities, and philanthropies stepped up
     funding for climate research in lieu of federal support in that period).
     That said, globally coordinated issues (like climate) suffer if a major
     player politicizes science.
Modern
     Suppression Still Exists:
 It would be wrong to assume modern times
     only face “soft” attacks. Cases like Chinese scientist Dr. Li Wenliang –
     reprimanded by police for warning about COVID-19 in Dec 2019 (he later
     died of the virus) – show authoritarian regimes still silence scientists
     directly, with severe consequences (

     under threat | Royal Society
). In Russia, climate scientist Alexander
     Solovyov was allegedly harassed for publishing inconvenient permafrost
     data. And in Iran, environmental scientists like Kavous Seyed-Emami died
     in custody in 2018 amid accusations of espionage. These echo the old
     patterns of persecution, just in a contemporary setting.
Social
     Media and AI:
 One new element tilting the landscape is technology
     itself. The 
same digital tools that empower science communication (open
     access journals, global collaboration) also enable rapid spread of
     anti-science propaganda
 (fake “experts” on YouTube, bots boosting
     conspiracy hashtags). As noted, falsehoods can outrun truths online (

     On Twitter, false news travels faster than true stories | MIT News |

) (

     Massachusetts Institute of Technology
). AI-generated content
     (“deepfakes”) may soon produce fake scientific papers or fake experts that
     are hard to distinguish from real, potentially supercharging
     disinformation. This is a fundamentally modern challenge that didn’t exist
     in Galileo’s era or even 1980.
Political
     Suppression – Then vs Now:
 Is it more severe today? In liberal
     democracies, one could argue it’s 
less
 severe: we no longer
     imprison scientists for ideas. But in some ways, 
the stakes are higher
     now
 – science is telling us inconvenient truths about our way of life
     (climate change, pandemics) that require big societal changes. This
     provokes strong counter-reactions. For example, the climate denial machine
     today is arguably better funded and organized than any historical science
     opposition movement. It operates mostly through persuasion rather than
     force, which can be insidious – winning over a portion of the public to
     actively resist science. In the past, an average citizen’s role in an
     “attack on science” (say, the Inquisition) was minimal; today, any person
     can join an online mob harassing a climate scientist or vaccine
     researcher. The 
democratization of attack capability
 is a modern
     twist.
Net
     Effect Over Time:
 The overall trajectory since the Enlightenment has
     been increasing acceptance of scientific authority in governance (e.g.,
     use of expert advisors) and public life – which is why recent attacks
     often feel like backlash against a largely pro-science status quo. We can
     see cycles: periods of progress (e.g., post-WWII science golden age)
     followed by pushback (1970s–80s rise of deregulation ideology, etc.).
     Right now, we are in a pushback phase on multiple fronts, even as science
     itself advances rapidly.
Public Knowledge Access – Then vs Now:
 In the
17th–19th centuries, scientific knowledge was confined to elites. An erroneous
idea (like phlogiston theory) might persist simply due to slow communication
and low literacy, not active suppression. Today, thanks to the internet,
accurate scientific information is in principle a click away for billions – a
huge positive change from the past. One might think this access would end
ignorance-driven attacks. However, the internet also gives a platform to 
anti-science
voices
, creating an information flood where laypeople struggle to discern
credible sources. Unlike the Enlightenment era, where increasing printed
knowledge generally aided the cause of science, the internet era is
double-edged: it amplifies 
both
 enlightenment and conspiracy. On
balance, access to knowledge is vastly higher today, which should help truth
prevail, but the 
noise-to-signal ratio is also higher
. As a result,
public access alone doesn’t guarantee scientific understanding; it must be
paired with education and media literacy.
Conclusion on Trends:
 Historical attacks on science
often came from religious dogma or totalitarian state dogma. Modern attacks
increasingly come from 
ideologically driven factions or economic interest
groups operating in open societies
 (where they use persuasion rather than
force). The magnitude of direct violence against scientists is lower in many
countries now, but the magnitude of 
information warfare against scientific
consensus is arguably greater
. We’ve moved from an era of index librorum
prohibitorum (lists of banned books) to an era of algorithm-driven echo
chambers that 
effectively
 serve to “ban” uncomfortable facts from
certain audiences. The form has shifted, but the effect – impeding the
acceptance of evidence-based knowledge – persists.
In summary, attacks on science 
continue
 but have
transformed: from burning books to burning up social media, from jailing
scientists to subjecting them to online abuse or funding cuts. While humanity’s
scientific knowledge has grown exponentially, so have the tools to challenge
and misrepresent that knowledge. Whether attacks are more severe now depends on
the metric: fewer scientists are burned at the stake (thankfully), but perhaps
more are 
burned out
 by the constant fighting against tides of
misinformation.

     spreaders accountable (akin to how libel laws work). This is tricky – one
     person’s misinformation might be another’s opinion – but a starting point
     is enforcing platform policies strictly (e.g., ban repeat anti-vax
     propagators) and perhaps class-action lawsuits against those profiting
     from false health claims. As precedent, families of COVID victims have
     explored suing conspiracists for wrongful death when medical advice was
     thwarted by lies. Even if rare, such cases could deter the most harmful
     actors.
International
     Collaboration:
 Just as scientists collaborate globally, a network of
     governments and NGOs committed to defending science could share best
     practices and coordinate. For instance, during COVID, an international
     task force on misinformation (involving WHO, tech companies, and
     governments) was proposed to rapidly quash dangerous rumors – something to
     formalize for future pandemics or climate emergencies.
Resilience
     within Science:
 The scientific community should also address internal
     issues that attacks exploit. For example, when a few cases of fraud occur,
     they are blown up to tarnish all science. By improving reproducibility and
     ethical oversight, science can leave less ammo for critics. Also,
     diversity and inclusion in science can help: a more representative
     scientific workforce may earn trust more broadly and avoid perceptions of
     a distant “elite.”
