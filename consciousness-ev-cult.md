David Chalmers, and the Hard Problem of Consciousness
Chalmers (1995, 2022) has significantly influenced the philosophy of mind by distinguishing between the "easy problems" of consciousness—such as perception, information processing, and cognitive functions—and the "hard problem," which concerns subjective experience or qualia. 
While cognitive neuroscience and computational models attempt to explain the easy problems, they fail to address why physical processes give rise to first-person subjective experience. Chalmers (2022) further extends this inquiry into the realm of virtual reality and digital consciousness in 
Reality+

Several major theories of consciousness have been proposed, each addressing different aspects of the phenomenon. However, none fully encapsulate the multi-layered, fractal nature of consciousness as modeled in Evolutionary Culturology.

Integrated Information Theory (IIT)
Tononi (2004) posits that consciousness emerges from a system’s capacity to integrate information, measured as Φ (phi). The higher the Φ value, the more irreducible and self-contained the conscious experience is.
•
 
Strengths: Provides a quantitative metric for consciousness and emphasizes integration as a crucial factor.
•
 
Limitations: Lacks a mechanism explaining why integration leads to consciousness.
•
 
Ev Cult Perspective: Law #1 (Integration Upwards) explains IIT’s principle as a case of hierarchical HOLON/parton structuring. However, integration alone is insufficient; Laws #2 and #3 (competition and command) must also be present.
Reference: 
Tononi, G. (2004). An information integration theory of consciousness. BMC Neuroscience, 5(1), 42.
________________________________________
Global Workspace Theory (GWT)
Baars (1988) describes consciousness as a “global workspace” where different cognitive processes compete to broadcast their information to the system as a whole.
•
 
Strengths: Explains attention, working memory, and how multiple brain regions collaborate to generate awareness.
•
 
Limitations: Does not explain self-awareness or the persistence of conscious states.
•
 
Ev Cult Perspective: Law #2 (Cooperation/Competition Sideways) aligns with GWT’s competition for workspace access. However, GWT does not account for hierarchical integration (Law #1) or recursive meta-cognition (Law #3).
Reference: 
Baars, B. J. (1988). A Cognitive Theory of Consciousness. Cambridge University Press.
________________________________________
Higher-Order Thought Theory (HOT)
Rosenthal (2005) argues that consciousness arises when a system has thoughts about its own thoughts, forming meta-cognition.
•
 
Strengths: Addresses self-awareness and differentiates between unconscious and conscious mental processes.
•
 
Limitations: Fails to explain how lower-level cognitive processes integrate into meta-cognitive thought.
•
 
Ev Cult Perspective: Law #3 (Command/Control Downwards) provides a more comprehensive view of HOT, explaining how higher-order HOLON/partons regulate lower-level thought processes recursively.
Reference: 
Rosenthal, D. (2005). Consciousness and Mind. Oxford University Press.
________________________________________
Attention Schema Theory (AST)
Graziano (2013) suggests that consciousness is an internal model of attention, helping organisms track their own focus and predict the mental states of others.
•
 
Strengths: Accounts for social cognition and predictive processing.
•
 
Limitations: Treats consciousness as solely an attention-regulation tool, neglecting self-awareness and integration.
•
 
Ev Cult Perspective: Law #1 (Integration Upwards) and Law #3 (Command Downwards) extend AST’s explanation, showing that attention schemas are only one component of a larger HOLON/parton structure governing consciousness.

Graziano, M. S. A. (2013). Consciousness and the Social Brain. Oxford University Press.
