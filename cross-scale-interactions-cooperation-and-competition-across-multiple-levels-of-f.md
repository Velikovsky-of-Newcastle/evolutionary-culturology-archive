Introduction
The three laws of Evolutionary Culturology describe how systems and their components function across multiple levels of organization. 
Law #1 states that systems integrate upwards into larger supersystems; Law #2 identifies that systems laterally cooperate and compete with other systems at the same scale level; and Law #3 describes how each system commands and controls its subsystems downward and inward. 
While Law #2 typically emphasizes lateral dynamics among systems at the same level, this essay contends that an expanded understanding is required: one that recognizes how cooperation and competition also occur across scale levels.
The problem we confront is that interactions between scale levels—such as those between an individual and society, or a cell and an organ—have often been underestimated or insufficiently theorized. Yet in real systems, especially complex adaptive ones, such interactions are routine and foundational. The failure of a subsystem can collapse an entire system (e.g. cancer in biology), while support from smaller scales can lead to emergence and resilience (e.g. immune system responses, cultural revolutions). Therefore, this essay proposes a refinement in understanding of Law #2 to include systemic cooperation and competition that occurs not only laterally, but vertically, across the nested stack of fractal HOLON/partons.

Section 1. Patterns of Cross-Scale Interaction
Cross-scale interactions can take many forms. Four major types can be identified: upward cooperation, downward cooperation, upward competition, and downward competition. 
In upward cooperation, a subsystem supports the function and integrity of a supersystem. 
In downward cooperation, a supersystem nurtures or enables its subsystems. 
Conversely, in upward competition, a subsystem disrupts or challenges its parent system—such as in the case of cancerous cells disrupting an organ. 
In downward competition, a supersystem exploits or suppresses a subsystem, as seen in authoritarian regimes stifling dissent.
These dynamics are observed across domains. In biology, mitochondria within cells demonstrate upward cooperation via endosymbiosis, while cancer illustrates upward competition. In sociology, citizens resisting unjust systems reflect upward competition; whereas democracies with strong civic education represent downward cooperation. In engineering, modular design reflects mutual enablement across scales. In culture, ideologies and memes can rise from individuals and subcultures to dominate national discourse, or conversely, be suppressed from above.

Section 2. Entangled Feedback Loops Across Scales
Cross-scale interactions do not occur in isolation. Instead, they form feedback loops that recursively affect system behavior. For example, societal norms (a meso-level phenomenon) influence individual behaviors (micro-level), which in turn aggregate to shift norms. When a large number of individuals protest, institutions may collapse or reform, illustrating upward competition and eventual re-integration.
These dynamics can escalate across scales. Minor competition at a lower level can cause tipping points that lead to macro-level transformation. For instance, gene editing technologies (from molecular to organismal levels) may bypass systemic feedback mechanisms and cause unanticipated effects on ecosystems or human health. Such insights emphasize the importance of modeling feedback across nested levels, not merely within them. Agent-based simulations and dynamic systems modeling are valuable tools for revealing these patterns.
