1. Past Efforts to Count the Number of Domains in Culture
The classification of knowledge has been a longstanding endeavor in human intellectual history... 
Various attempts have been made, to structure and categorize domains of knowledge, yet most have lacked a unified scientific framework that accounts for their fractal, hierarchical nature.
1.1 Historical Classifications of Knowledge
One of the earliest systematic attempts to classify knowledge was Aristotle’s division into 3 domains: 1. theoretical, 2. practical, and 3. productive sciences (Aristotle 335 BCE, trans. 1984). 
Later, medieval universities adopted the trivium (1. grammar, 2. rhetoric, and 3. logic) and quadrivium (1. arithmetic, 2. geometry, 3. music, and 4. astronomy) as foundational disciplines (McLaughlin, 2003). 
The Enlightenment saw a surge in taxonomic efforts, with scholars like Diderot and d’Alembert, structuring knowledge in their 
Encyclopédie
 (1765), which categorized human knowledge into three primary branches: 1. memory (history), 2. reason (philosophy), and 3. imagination (poetry, arts).
Despite these early attempts, none provided a systematic method to understand why cultural domains emerge or how they evolve hierarchically over time.
1.2 Modern Classification Systems
The Dewey Decimal System (Dewey, 1876) organizes knowledge into ten main classes, which are:
1.
 
000 – General works, Computer Science, and Information

100 – Philosophy and Psychology
3.
 
200 – Religion
4.
 
300 – Social Sciences
5.
 
400 – Language
6.
 
500 – Science
7.
 
600 – Technology
8.
 
700 – Arts and Recreation
9.
 
800 – Literature
10.
 
900 – History and Geography
Similarly, the Universal Decimal Classification (Otlet, 1934) refines knowledge into ten primary divisions: 1. General Knowledge, 2. Philosophy & Psychology, 3. Religion, 4. Social Sciences, 5. Language, 6. Natural Sciences & Mathematics, 7. Technology & Applied Sciences, 8. Arts & Recreation, 9. Literature, 10. History & Geography, with hierarchical subdomains expanding into over 70,000 subdivisions.
UNESCO’s classification of disciplines (UNESCO, 1982) defines six major domains: natural sciences, engineering and technology, medical sciences, agricultural sciences, social sciences, and humanities. These are further broken down into 46 disciplines, which include sub-disciplines.
Although these classification systems acknowledge a hierarchical structure, they do not explain why knowledge differentiates and scales in a fractal manner.
1.3 Scientific and Interdisciplinary Taxonomies
Efforts to classify knowledge scientifically have emerged in the past century. Biglan (1973) categorized knowledge into three broad clusters based on disciplinary differences: hard/soft, pure/applied, and life/non-life sciences. 
The OECD’s Frascati Manual (OECD, 2015) introduced a six-domain structure for research domains: 1. Natural sciences, 2. Engineering and technology, 3. Medical sciences, 4. Agricultural sciences, 5. Social sciences, 6. Humanities, subdivided into 42 disciplines and hundreds of sub-disciplines.
More recently, interdisciplinary frameworks like Mode 1 and Mode 2 knowledge production (Gibbons et al., 1994) have explored the dynamic, evolving nature of knowledge domains. 
However, none of these models explicitly address the fractal scaling of cultural knowledge across different levels, nor do they provide a mechanism for scientifically unifying these domains.

E.O. Wilson (1998) famously articulated the problem of consilience—the fragmentation of human knowledge across the natural sciences, social sciences, and humanities.

David L. Hull’s

Integral Theory (Wilber, 2000) attempted a holistic synthesis, but remains a philosophical model, rather than a scientific one.
These models all attempted unification, but without identifying a universal structuring principle, leading to their fragmentation and conceptual limitations.
