Introduction
In George Eliot's classic novel 
"Middlemarch" (1871-2)
 the character of Dorothea Brooke poignantly encapsulates a cognitive pitfall that permeates human judgment: the propensity to form opinions based on insufficient information. An excerpt from the novel, as Sir James and Dorothea walk along:
“You have your own opinion about everything, Miss Brooke, and it is always a good opinion.”
What answer was possible to such stupid complimenting?
“Do you know, I envy you that,” Sir James said, as they continued walking at the rather brisk pace set by Dorothea.
“I don’t quite understand what you mean.”
“Your power of forming an opinion. I can form an opinion of persons. I know when I like people. But about other matters, do you know, I have often a difficulty in deciding. One hears very sensible things said on opposite sides.”
“Or that seem sensible. Perhaps we don’t always discriminate between sense and nonsense.”
Dorothea felt that she was rather rude.
“Exactly,” said Sir James. “But you seem to have the power of discrimination.”
“On the contrary, I am often unable to decide. But that is from ignorance. The right conclusion is there all the same, though I am unable to see it.”

Cognitive Biases and Insufficient Evidence
Empirical studies have demonstrated various 
cognitive biases
 that contribute to flawed human decision-making.
For instance, 
confirmation bias
 leads individuals to seek out information that confirms pre-existing beliefs while ignoring disconfirming evidence (Nickerson, 1998). This directly contravenes the Bayesian principle of updating beliefs, based on all available information.
Moreover, heuristics like "availability" and "representativeness" bias our judgment by skewing the likelihood estimation in Bayes' Theorem (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). People overestimate the frequency of events that are easily recallable, and they often judge the probability of an event based on how similar it is to a prototype in their minds. In short, people are terrible at thinking.
