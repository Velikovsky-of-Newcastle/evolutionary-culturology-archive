Definitions, derived from Challoner (2024, Parts 1-4)
1. Agency: The ability of an organism or group of organisms to make its own decisions and act independently.
2. Amensalism: An ecological interaction in which one party is harmed, whilst the benefits or harms to the other are negligible.
3. Antibiosis: An ecological interaction in which at least one party is harmed.
4. Behavioural predispositions: States of mind which do not necessarily lead to immediate action, but which prepare us to act when the opportunity to satisfy a need arises.
5. Bottom-up process: A process in which followers choose a leader thought to be best qualified to coordinate their activities.
6. Causal diagram: A visual representation of the causal relationships between variables in a system, showing the direction of influence.
7. Closed system: A system in which other potential influences are excluded or held constant to identify what a particular generative mechanism does on its own.
8. Cultural manipulation: The deliberate alteration of a culture’s values, norms, and beliefs through advertising, propaganda, etc.
9. Culture: Information comprising values, norms, beliefs, knowledge, and symbols held in the minds of individuals, and the behavior these generate. When a group of people share these, it is referred to as "their culture". The group behaving similarly is referred to as "a culture".
10. Enculturement: A term coined by the author to describe the way in which cultural components or new values, norms, and beliefs are communicated from the individual to others, forming a part of their culture.
11. Extractive institutions: Institutions that extract wealth from a society for the benefit of a minority or external agents.
12. Genotype: The genetic constitution of an organism.
13. Human holon: Any individual, group of people, or group of groups working together with a common purpose.
14. Inclusive institutions: Institutions that share the wealth of a society equitably with the members of that society.
15. Institution: A human holon, particularly a large, established, and influential one, with a structure, culture, and function.
16. Internalised: Assimilated into the unconscious mind.
17. Meme: Same as a cultural component.
18. Memeplex: Same as a culture or part of a culture comprising several associated values, norms, and/or beliefs.
19. Morphogenetic Cycle: A model of the interactions between culture, social structure, and individual agents proposed by the English sociologist, Margaret Archer.
20. Multi-level selection: A process of natural selection that occurs at each biological level, from genome to ecosystem, with stronger selection processes at lower levels often undermining those at higher levels.
21. Natural selection: Influences of the environment on the phenotype that determine its success in propagating its genome. Together with random mutation, this is one of the factors causing evolution.
22. Operant conditioning: A method of education using satisfiers and contra-satisfiers to alter behavior.
23. Reflexivity: The regular exercise of the mental ability to consider oneself in relation to their social contexts and vice versa, leading to internal conversations that mediate the influence of structural and cultural factors on social action.
24. Schema (pl. schemata): Mental structures organizing information on a particular topic and the relationships between them.
25. Social Learning: The copying of values, norms, or beliefs from an apparently successful role model.
26. Stratification of nature: The hierarchical arrangement of nature's strata, with higher strata depending on the existence of lower ones.
27. Transitive object of science: Scientific knowledge held by people, which is mutable and changes with time and circumstances.
28. Vertical explanation: The explanation of greater generative mechanisms, holons, or systems in terms of lesser ones from the stratum below.
29. Vicious circle: A positive feedback loop in which harms steadily increase in magnitude.
30. Virtuous circle: A positive feedback loop in which benefits steadily increase in magnitude.
 
Literature Review 
Summary of 
A Theory of Society Derived from the Principles of Systems, Parts 1-4  
(
Challoner (2024) 
John A. Challoner's four-part series, "
A Theory of Society Derived from the Principles of Systems
," provides a comprehensive exploration of societal dynamics through the lens of systems science, psychology, ecology, and evolutionary principles. 
This literature review synthesizes the key concepts, principles, and theoretical underpinnings presented across the four parts.
Part 1: Foundations of Systems Theory
The first part introduces the foundational concepts of systems theory, as they apply to social structures. Challoner emphasizes the hierarchical nature of systems, wherein each system is composed of subsystems and contributes to larger systems. This part outlines the importance of understanding the interactions and relationships between different levels of systems, referred to as holons. The notion of generative mechanisms, which are emergent properties arising from the interaction of subsystems, is central to this discussion. These mechanisms explain the behaviors and outputs that cannot be predicted by examining individual components alone.
Part 2: Critical Realism and Social Morphogenesis
In the second part, Challoner draws on the works of Roy Bhaskar and Margaret Archer to delve into the philosophical underpinnings of his theory. Bhaskar’s critical realism, which combines transcendental realism and critical naturalism, provides a framework for understanding the stratified nature of reality and the role of generative mechanisms. Archer’s concept of social morphogenesis, which describes the cyclical process of social structure shaping individual agency and vice versa, is explored in depth. This section introduces key principles, such as emergent generative mechanisms as the basis of causality and the stratification of sciences based on rootedness and emergence.
As an interesting isomorphy, 
Ev Cult
 (2016-2023) also draws on 
Structuration Theory
, or 
the Agency/Structure Duality
, as per Archer and Giddens.
Part 3: Cultural Evolution and Reflexivity
Part three expands on the dynamics of cultural evolution, and the role of reflexivity in social change. Challoner discusses how cultural elements, or `memes', propagate through social systems, influencing and being influenced by individual behaviors and societal structures. Reflexivity, as described by Margaret Archer, is the process through which individuals critically evaluate their social contexts and internal dialogues, leading to changes in social action. The integration of psychological defense mechanisms and their impact on cultural evolution are also examined, highlighting the complex interplay between individual cognition and broader cultural trends.
Part 4: Applications and Implications
The final part of the series applies the theoretical concepts to real-world social phenomena. Challoner discusses the practical implications of his theory for understanding social pathologies, institutional dynamics, and the potential for societal transformation. The principles of cultural manipulation, inclusive versus extractive institutions, and the role of human holons in societal coordination are explored. Challoner also addresses the challenges of applying scientific methodologies to social sciences, emphasizing the importance of empirical testing and the identification of generative mechanisms through natural observations and case studies.
Summary
John A. Challoner's "A Theory of Society Derived from the Principles of Systems" (Parts 1-4) provides a robust framework for analyzing social structures and cultural evolution. 
By integrating systems science with philosophical and psychological insights, Challoner offers a comprehensive approach to understanding the complex interactions that drive societal change. The theory’s emphasis on hierarchical systems, generative mechanisms, and reflexivity provides valuable tools for both theoretical exploration and practical application in social sciences.
