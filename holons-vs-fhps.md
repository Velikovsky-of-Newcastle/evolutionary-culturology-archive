Introduction
Arthur Koestler's introduction of the "holon" in his 1967 work
The Ghost in the Machine
 provided a foundational concept for
understanding the hierarchical structure of complex systems. 
Holons, as
described by Koestler, are entities that exist as both autonomous wholes and
dependent parts within larger systems. This duality allows holons to maintain
their identity while simultaneously participating in a broader context, a
principle that has influenced numerous fields, from biology to organizational
theory.
In contrast, the concept of 
fractal HOLON/partons
,
introduced within the framework of the meta-meta-science of Evolutionary
Culturology (2016), expands on Koestler’s idea by integrating principles from fractal
geometry, and particle physics. 
Specifically, 
fractal HOLON/partons 
are inspired
by Mandelbrot’s work on fractals, and Feynman’s introduction of partons as
subcomponents of protons and neutrons. 
This paper examines how these two
concepts align and diverge, providing a comprehensive analysis of their roles
in modern scientific thought.
Theoretical Perspective
Koestler's holons
Koestler’s (1967) holons are defined by their position within a
hierarchical system. Each holon is both a whole entity and a part of a larger
structure, functioning autonomously yet remaining connected to and influenced
by its context. This idea was pivotal in explaining complex systems, in which
every level of organization, from the smallest to the largest, exhibits both
independence and interdependence.
Holons are governed by what Koestler described as the
"Janus effect," named after the two-faced Roman god Janus,
symbolizing their dual role as both parts and wholes. This perspective was
groundbreaking in understanding the dynamics of hierarchical systems, from
biological organisms to human organizations.
Fractal HOLON/partons in Evolutionary Culturology
Fractal HOLON/partons build upon Koestler’s (1967) concept
by introducing the element of fractality, derived from Mandelbrot’s work on
fractals. In Evolutionary Culturology, 
fractal HOLON/partons

fundamental units of both (1) systems, and (2) units of culture, the latter representing
ideas, processes, and products, that have self-similar structure and function (via 3 Laws) across different scales. 
In the domain of culture, this means that the same patterns
can be observed whether one is examining a small cultural unit, like a word - or
a large one, like an entire narrative. 
However Ev Cult's 
fractal HOLON/partons 
apply
across Nature, Culture, and Technology, namely: all domains.
Additionally, the term "parton," borrowed from
Feynman’s (1975) work in particle physics, emphasizes the idea that these units
are the building blocks of larger phenomena.

Similarities Between Koestler's `Holons’ and Ev Cult's 
fractal
HOLON/partons
1. Duality as Parts and Wholes: Both holons and 
fractal
HOLON/partons
 function as entities that are both independent and dependent.
They maintain their own integrity while contributing to larger systems.
2. Hierarchical Structure: Koestler’s (1967) holons and Ev
Cult’s (2016) fractal HOLON/partons both operate within hierarchical
frameworks. In both models, smaller units aggregate to form more complex
systems, with each level of organization influencing and being influenced by
the levels above and below.
3. Systemic Integration: Both concepts emphasize the
integration of parts into wholes, highlighting the interconnectedness of
systems, whether biological, organizational, or cultural.
Differences Between holons, and 
fractal HOLON/partons
1. Fractality vs. Hierarchy: While Koestler’s holons are
primarily hierarchical, fractal HOLON/partons emphasize
fractality—self-similarity across different scales. This fractal nature allows
for the same patterns to recur at different levels of complexity, a concept not
explicitly present in Koestler’s holons.
2. Origins and Influences: Koestler’s holons were influenced
by his interest in biology and systems theory. In contrast, fractal
HOLON/partons are a synthesis of ideas from multiple fields: Mandelbrot’s
fractals (1975, 1982), Koestler’s holons (1967, 1978), and Feynman’s partons (1975,
2005), reflecting a broader interdisciplinary approach.
3. Application Across Domains: Koestler’s holons were
initially applied to biological and organizational systems, whereas fractal
HOLON/partons are used to analyze cultural phenomena across all domains,
including language, art, technology, and social systems.
