Mathematical Measures of Creativity
 (Boden, 1990; Schmidhuber, 2008)
Margaret Boden (1990)
proposed a framework for understanding creativity through three key dimensions:
combinational creativity, exploratory creativity, and transformational
creativity
. Combinational creativity involves generating novel ideas by
merging existing concepts in new ways, which aligns with the principle of
hybrid vigor in Evolutionary Culturology. Exploratory creativity involves
navigating existing conceptual spaces to uncover novel insights, while
transformational creativity modifies or expands these spaces altogether,
enabling paradigm shifts. Boden’s work emphasizes the structured nature of
creativity and its reliance on systematic cognitive and cultural processes.
Juergen Schmidhuber
(2008) introduced a computational approach to creativity, defining it in terms
of 
compression progress
. According to Schmidhuber, creative processes
seek to identify patterns in information that lead to more efficient data
encoding, making knowledge more compact and meaningful. He proposed that
novelty emerges from minimizing redundancy while maximizing information gain, a
principle that aligns with Shannon entropy and Kullback-Leibler (K-L)
divergence. Schmidhuber's research suggests that creativity is driven by a
balance between predictability and surprise, where ideas that optimize
compression while maintaining meaningful structure are more likely to be
perceived as innovative.
These mathematical
perspectives provide a foundation for quantifying creativity in scientific and
cultural contexts. By combining Boden’s conceptual models with Schmidhuber’s
formal computational framework, we can derive a systematic approach to measuring
creativity through semantic distance, usefulness, and unexpected information
gain. These approaches integrate well with the Hybrid Vigor Score (HVS),
offering a mathematical basis for evaluating how interdisciplinary ideas
contribute to meaningful innovation.

In the

applicability. The Geneplore Model suggests that creativity is not entirely

the cognitive underpinnings of hybrid vigor in cultural evolution.
5. Measuring Hybrid
Vigor in Creativity
Charles Darwin introduced the concept of 
hybrid vigor
, also known as 
heterosis
, in the context of biological evolution. In his work 
The Effects of Cross and Self Fertilization in the Vegetable Kingdom
 (1876), Darwin demonstrated that crossbreeding between genetically diverse individuals often leads to offspring that exhibit superior traits, such as increased size, fertility, and resistance to disease. 
He observed that self-fertilization and inbreeding, by contrast, frequently resulted in weakened or less viable progeny. This principle of hybrid vigor suggests that diversity—whether genetic or conceptual—enhances adaptability, resilience, and overall performance. In the context of creativity, hybrid vigor operates similarly: when ideas from distinct domains are combined, they often yield more novel, useful, and surprising results than those constrained within a single domain. By applying Darwin’s insights to interdisciplinary innovation, we can better understand how diverse intellectual recombinations generate stronger, more impactful ideas.

8. The Mathematical
Science of Creativity
Mathematically
quantifying creativity requires assessing how novel, useful, and surprising an
idea is. Various computational and statistical models have been developed to
measure these dimensions.
Novelty
 is typically measured using 
semantic
distance models
 such as 
Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA)
, which
computes the relative distance between an idea and pre-existing knowledge
within a given conceptual space. For instance, a patent that references fields
rarely cited together would be considered highly novel. Another approach
involves 
outlier detection methods
, where ideas that deviate
significantly from a dataset of existing concepts are marked as more novel.
Entropy-based measures, such as 
Shannon entropy
, can also quantify
unpredictability in data, with higher entropy signifying greater novelty.
Usefulness
 is assessed by evaluating an idea’s practical
impact. In machine learning, this might involve 
task success rates
,
where a new algorithm is tested against benchmark problems to measure
improvements in efficiency, accuracy, or cost-effectiveness. In cultural
evolution, the 
adoption rate
 of an idea can serve as a proxy for
usefulness, as seen in the diffusion of innovations model. Historical analyses
of scientific breakthroughs, such as the spread of Einstein’s theories, reveal
how usefulness correlates with rapid integration into academic and
technological applications.
Surprisingness
 is effectively captured by 
Kullback-Leibler
(K-L) Divergence
, a statistical measure of how much an idea deviates from
prior expectations. If a concept significantly alters a probability
distribution—such as an unexpected scientific discovery shifting the
consensus—then it is highly surprising. Bayesian Information Gain models are
also used to quantify how much uncertainty is reduced by an idea, with larger
reductions indicating higher levels of surprise. A classic example is the
discovery of quantum mechanics, which dramatically revised classical physics
models.
By integrating these
mathematical tools into Evolutionary Culturology, we can create a comprehensive
framework for assessing creative output in a rigorous and replicable manner.
