Evolutionary Culturology and Critical Realism: A Comparative Analysis
Abstract
This paper explores the relationship between Evolutionary
Culturology (Ev Cult) and Critical Realism (CR), analyzing their
points of intersection, fundamental differences, and broader implications for
scientific and philosophical inquiry. Both frameworks reject simplistic
positivism and emphasize the existence of deep structures that underlie
observable reality. However, while Bhaskar’s Critical Realism is
primarily concerned with providing a philosophical foundation for scientific
realism and the identification of causal mechanisms, Evolutionary
Culturology extends these principles into a universal meta-meta-scientific framework,
applicable across all domains and disciplines. This paper argues that while Critical Realism
offers valuable ontological and epistemological insights, Evolutionary
Culturology provides a more comprehensive, evolution-based model that unifies
knowledge across disciplines and domains.

1. Introduction
The search for robust theoretical frameworks that explain
the structure and evolution of reality across disciplines has led to the
development of multiple philosophical and scientific models. 
Among these, the philosophy of Critical
Realism (CR), developed by Roy Bhaskar (1975, 1979) - and the meta-meta-science of Evolutionary Culturology (Ev
Cult), discovered by Velikovsky of Newcastle (2016), emerge as two particularly significant
approaches. 
Both frameworks reject reductionist positivism and extreme
interpretivism, recognizing the importance of deep structures and
emergent processes that influence and shape observable phenomena.
Critical Realism, first formulated in the 1970s, is a
philosophy of science that provides an ontological basis for
understanding the stratified nature of reality and the causal mechanisms
that underpin both scientific and social phenomena. Its central tenets
emphasize the existence of multiple layers of reality, only some of
which are directly observable, and the necessity of uncovering hidden causal
structures to attain genuine scientific knowledge. By distinguishing between ontology
(what exists) and epistemology (how we know what exists), Bhaskar
aimed to correct the epistemic fallacy, which is the tendency to
conflate knowledge with reality itself.
In contrast, Evolutionary Culturology (Ev Cult),
developed in the early 21st century (2016), offers a meta-meta-scientific framework
that applies evolutionary principles across all domains.

2. Foundations of Critical Realism (CR)
2.1 Bhaskar’s Core Principles
Bhaskar’s 
Critical Realism
 is built upon several fundamental
principles that distinguish it from both positivism and constructivist
epistemologies. The most central of these is the idea of stratified
ontology, which asserts that reality consists of three distinct but
interconnected layers: the empirical, the actual, and the real.
Table 0. Bhaskar's Domains of Depth Ontology (Adapted from Bhaskar 1975, p. 13)
In Bhaskar's ontology, the empirical level consists of what human beings
directly observe and experience. This is the domain of sensory perception
and recorded data. However, Bhaskar argues that limiting science to this level
alone leads to empirical reductionism, in which only observable
phenomena are considered real.
The actual level consists of events and processes
that occur, regardless of whether they are observed. Many events in reality
happen without human perception, meaning that not all actual occurrences
become part of empirical knowledge.
The real level represents the underlying causal
structures and mechanisms that give rise to events at the actual level.
According to Bhaskar, scientific inquiry should be aimed at uncovering these deep
structures, as they are responsible for generating the observable phenomena
at the empirical level.
In addition to stratified ontology, Bhaskar introduced the
concept of causal mechanisms, which are forces, tendencies, or
structures that operate independently of human perception. These
mechanisms, found at the real level of reality, may or may not always be
actualized as observable events, but they remain significant drivers of change
and development within a system.
Another major tenet of Critical Realism is the rejection of
the epistemic fallacy, which occurs when reality is reduced to what can
be known about it. Bhaskar insists that ontology (what exists) is distinct
from epistemology (how we know what exists), emphasizing that scientific
progress depends on acknowledging the independent existence of structures
beyond human perception.
Finally, Bhaskar’s work also emphasizes emergence and
structural organization. He argues that wholes have emergent properties
that cannot be reduced to their constituent parts. This idea is
particularly important in systems theory and social science, where interactions
at lower levels give rise to higher-order phenomena that possess unique
characteristics.
2.2 Application of CR in Social and Natural Sciences
Critical Realism is widely applied in both the natural
sciences and social sciences, serving as an alternative to positivist
empiricism. In the social sciences, for instance, it has been used to
analyze social structures, power relations, and institutional dynamics
by uncovering the deep mechanisms that shape social behavior. 
In the
natural sciences, it provides a realist approach to scientific inquiry,
rejecting both reductionist materialism and subjective idealism
in favor of an ontology that acknowledges the existence of non-observable
causal forces.
This theoretical foundation allows Critical Realism to be a powerful
tool for scientific inquiry, offering a framework for understanding complex
causality, emergence, and the role of deep structures in shaping reality.

4.2 Rejection of Positivism and Interpretivism
Another area of convergence is their mutual rejection of
reductionist positivism and extreme constructivism. Bhaskar critiques empiricism,
arguing that science must uncover hidden causal structures rather than
merely observe surface-level regularities.

4.3 Understanding Causal Structures
Bhaskar’s causal mechanisms and Ev Cult’s evolutionary

explains causality in terms of emergent properties and underlying tendencies,
Ev Cult extends this by incorporating variation and selection processes,
showing how information, behaviors, and systems evolve over time. Both
approaches agree that events are not merely linear or deterministic but
arise from interactions among multiple layers of structure and agency.

5. Divergence: Why Ev Cult Goes Beyond Critical Realism
5.1 Expanding Beyond Scientific Realism
While Bhaskar’s Critical Realism serves as a
philosophical framework for the natural and social sciences, it does not
claim to provide a universal model for all domains of reality.
Evolutionary Culturology, by contrast, extends its explanatory power across
all disciplines, including physics, chemistry, biology, psychology,
information systems, and cultural evolution. Ev Cult is not just a
philosophy of science—it is a meta-meta-science that integrates scientific
realism, evolutionary theory, and systems thinking into a single
overarching paradigm.
5.2 Evolution as a Core Explanatory Principle
Critical Realism explains change through emergent
properties and causal mechanisms, but it does not provide a systematic
model for how and why systems evolve. Ev Cult fills this gap by applying evolutionary
selection principles to all forms of information and organization. Unlike
Bhaskar’s static ontology of reality layers, Ev Cult treats reality as an
evolving, self-organizing system, where fractal HOLON/partons interact
through selection pressures over time. This dynamism makes Ev Cult
not just a broader framework but a more powerful tool for explaining
long-term change in any system.
5.3 Causality: Mechanisms vs. Evolutionary Selection
Bhaskar’s causal realism suggests that unseen
mechanisms govern the behavior of objects and events, meaning that science
must uncover these hidden forces. Evolutionary Culturology agrees with
this premise but extends it, arguing that causal explanations must also
account for selection processes at multiple levels. Where Critical Realism
relies on emergence and tendencies, Ev Cult explains why certain
tendencies persist and spread, while others disappear, based on evolutionary
fitness.
