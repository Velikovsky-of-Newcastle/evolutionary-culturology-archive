Introduction
In the quest for consilience—the unity of knowledge—scientists and scholars have often sought ways to draw connections between seemingly disparate fields. Evolutionary Culturology (Ev Cult) provides a systematic approach to this challenge, presenting the Universal Domain Map (UDM) as a tool to compare and contrast domains of culture. 
By mapping these domains through their shared structures and processes, Ev Cult opens the possibility of applying solutions from one domain to another. This approach is particularly valuable in addressing complex global challenges, as solutions from one domain may enhance or replace inefficient methods in others.
The structural mappings between domains are essential in this context. Such mappings involve identifying patterns, isomorphisms, and analogous frameworks across various fields—whether in science, technology, art, or social systems. 
By leveraging Ev Cult’s fractal HOLON/parton framework, this paper explores the methodologies that allow for the identification and transfer of solutions across these domains.
Theoretical Perspective
Evolutionary Culturology posits that all domains of culture—whether they pertain to mathematics, biology, physics, or art—are composed of units called fractal HOLON/partons.
These units adhere to three laws: they integrate upwards into larger systems, cooperate and compete laterally with other units on the same level, and command and control smaller units within them.
In this context, structural mappings between domains require identifying how fractal HOLON/partons in one domain correspond to those in another. 
These mappings reveal underlying similarities, enabling the transfer of successful solutions across domains. This theory aligns with interdisciplinary research methods such as systems theory, analogical reasoning, and evolutionary algorithms, which help us find patterns and isomorphisms across fields.

1.2 Isomorphisms and Analogical Reasoning
Identifying isomorphisms—shared structures that perform analogous functions in different domains—allows for a deeper understanding of cross-domain relations. 
For example, feedback loops in control systems in engineering may parallel self-regulation in ecosystems, enabling the application of engineering principles to biological conservation.
1.3 Systems Theory and Cybernetics
Systems theory and Cybernetics provide a robust framework for comparing different domains, focusing on how systems regulate, adapt, and process information. 
By comparing control mechanisms across domains (e.g., nervous systems in biology and regulatory systems in economics), insights from one field can inform the development of solutions in others.
1.4 Evolutionary Algorithms and Cross-Domain Optimization
Evolutionary algorithms (e.g., SVT, ST, BV-SR, etc.) can simulate processes of adaptation and optimization across domains. By viewing each domain as an evolving system, we can apply optimization techniques from one domain to another, such as using genetic algorithms in computing to solve resource allocation problems in economics.

Using dimensionality reduction techniques such as principal component analysis (PCA), we can simplify complex systems to their most important dimensions. 
This allows us to superimpose domains and explore how shared axes—such as time, complexity, and space—can inform cross-domain problem-solving.
