2. Some Brief Historical Context: Cybernetics
The roots of Cybernetics lie in mid-20th-century 
Systems Theory (von Bertalanffy 1950)
, and were primarily developed by Norbert Wiener (1948) to describe feedback and control mechanisms in machines and organisms. 
Wiener’s work emphasized the importance of self-regulating systems that adapt to their environments through feedback loops—a principle later extended to domains such as biology, sociology, and AI.
In 1988, Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi introduced The Systems Model of Creativity, a groundbreaking framework that applied Systems Thinking to cultural creativity. 
This model conceptualized creativity as emerging from interactions between the Domain, the creative Agent, and the evaluative Field. 
Over time, Csikszentmihalyi refined the Systems Model of Creativity, retitling it:
•
 
DIFi (Domain-Individual-Field Interaction, 1988),
•
 
DPFi (Domain-Person-Field Interaction, 2006), and
•
 
DAFi (Domain-Agent-Field Interaction, 2014+), acknowledging that Agents could include groups or AI systems.
 (In sum, renaming, the: `Individual', `Person', `Agent'.)

Definitions of Key Terms Used in this paper:
1.
 
Cybernetics
o
 
The interdisciplinary study of systems, communication, and control, focusing on feedback loops, regulation, and adaptation in biological, mechanical, and cultural systems (Wiener, 1948). Key theorists in Cybernetics include:
Norbert Wiener: The pioneer of cybernetics.
Ross Ashby: Known for his contributions to System Theory, and the "Law of Requisite Variety" (the variety in a control-system must 
match 
that of the system it controls).
Stafford Beer: Applied cybernetics to management, and developed the Viable Systems Model for organizations.
Gregory Bateson: Integrated cybernetics into anthropology, psychology, and communication theory.
2.
 
Feedback Loop
o
 
A process in which outputs of a system are fed back as inputs, allowing for regulation, adaptation, and control (e.g., positive and negative feedback).
 
3.
 
Self-Regulation
o
 
A system's ability to maintain balance and adapt to internal or external changes through feedback mechanisms.
4.
 
Information Flow
o
 
The movement of information within and between systems, enabling communication and decision-making processes.
5.
 
System Boundaries
o
 
The delineation of subsystems (e.g., Domain, Agent, Field) within a larger system, allowing for focused analysis of interactions.
6.
 
Emergence
o
 
A property where complex outcomes arise from the interactions of simpler system components, producing something greater than the sum of its parts.
