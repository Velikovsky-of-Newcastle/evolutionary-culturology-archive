Theoretical Perspectives
1. Leibniz's "Windowless Monads"
Leibniz (1714) proposed that the universe is composed of `monads', indivisible and self-contained metaphysical entities. 
Each `monad' is a reflection of the entire universe, but operates independently of others, governed by a pre-established harmony set by "God". 
Monads do not interact directly with each other, as they are "windowless," meaning they neither receive external influence, nor exert influence on others. This framework addresses metaphysical questions of substance and causality, positing a universe where harmony and order are maintained without direct interaction between the fundamental units.
(...We should cut Leibniz some slack, he was working in this stuff a long time ago, when "God" was imagined to be a real thing.)
Bohm's Implicate Order
David Bohm (1980) introduced the concept of 
the implicate order, 
to explain the interconnectedness observed in quantum mechanics, and to provide a holistic framework for understanding reality. 
In Bohm's view, reality consists of two orders: the implicate order, where all points in space-time are enfolded into one another, and the explicate order, the manifest reality that unfolds from the implicate. 
Unlike Leibniz's 
monads
, the implicate order allows for a continuous, dynamic process where all parts are interconnected, and causality is non-local, operating across space-time in a holistic manner.
