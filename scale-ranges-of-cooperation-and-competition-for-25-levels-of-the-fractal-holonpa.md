Viruses: Competition Across Multiple Scales
Viruses are renowned for their ability to infect and, in
many cases, kill cells. However - their impact is far more extensive than just
cellular destruction… A virus can spread within tissues, compromising the
function of entire organs. For example, a virus like hepatitis can damage the
liver (an organ), while the influenza virus can affect the respiratory system
(an organ system). The scale of competition extends further when a virus
spreads throughout an organism, potentially leading to death.
Beyond the individual organism scale, viruses can devastate
entire populations, or even whole species. The 1918 influenza pandemic, for
instance, infected one-third of the global population - and led to millions of
deaths - demonstrating how a virus can compete on the scale of a population.
Similarly, the HIV/AIDS epidemic has had profound effects on human populations,
shaping social, economic, and health policies worldwide.
In some cases, the impact of viruses extends to entire
ecosystems. For instance, viruses that affect keystone species—organisms that
have a disproportionate impact on their environment—can trigger cascading
effects throughout an ecosystem. The collapse of one species due to a viral
outbreak can alter predator-prey dynamics, plant pollination, and even nutrient
cycles, illustrating the far-reaching competitive influence of viruses, across
multiple scales of the 
fractal HOLON/parton
 hierarchy.
Viruses: Cooperation Across Multiple Scales
While viruses are often viewed as purely destructive agents,
they also play cooperative roles within ecosystems and evolutionary processes!
Some viruses are beneficial to their hosts, contributing to genetic diversity
and adaptation. For instance, bacteriophages—viruses that infect bacteria—can
help regulate bacterial populations in ecosystems, preventing overgrowth and
maintaining ecological balance.
Moreover, viruses have contributed to the evolution of their
hosts. A striking example is the role of retroviruses in the evolution of
mammals, including humans. Approximately 8% of the human genome is composed of
endogenous retroviruses, remnants of ancient viral infections that have been
integrated into our DNA. Some of these viral genes have been co-opted by the
host, and serve important functions, such as the formation of the placenta in
mammals. This illustrates how viruses can cooperate across scales by
contributing to the genetic and evolutionary makeup of organisms, and by
extension, species.
