On the Evolutionary History of the Domain of Narrative
 
In considering the evolutionary history of storytelling, it’s unlikely that all the functions of narrative emerged at once. 
Early humans in the Pleistocene - and particularly those in the early phases of cognitive evolution - likely used storytelling in its simplest forms. For these early humans, storytelling probably first emerged as a tool for survival and social cohesion. In small hunter-gatherer groups, stories would have played a crucial role in transmitting essential information about the environment, such as how to find food, avoid predators, or navigate dangerous landscapes. These early narratives were probably a mix of cognitive simulation—allowing members of the group to mentally rehearse survival scenarios—and cultural transmission, passing down crucial survival strategies and shared experiences across generations.
Storytelling at this stage was also likely to serve a role in social cohesion, reinforcing group identity and shared values. Early human groups were tightly knit, and stories would help maintain social bonds, promoting cooperation and trust among group members. The emotional and moral dimensions of storytelling, such as using narratives to regulate emotions or instill early moral lessons, may have emerged later, evolving in parallel with human cognitive and social complexity.
The more abstract functions of storytelling—such as entertainment, sexual selection, or memory consolidation—likely developed as communication technologies and cognitive abilities advanced. For instance, as language evolved into more sophisticated forms, the ability to create complex narratives likely became a marker of intelligence and creativity, playing a role in sexual selection by displaying cognitive fitness. 
Over time, as human societies grew larger and more complex, storytelling began to serve broader collective functions, such as shaping national identities, creating moral frameworks, and even fostering political propaganda, in more organized societies.
Thus, from a caveperson’s perspective in the early Pleistocene era, storytelling was primarily a basic tool for 
survival, cooperation, and knowledge transmission
. 
The wider functions we now associate with modern storytelling—like entertainment, moral education, or global influence—evolved and emerged more gradually, alongside human cognitive and social development, and were influenced by the emergence of new communication technologies, from the oral tradition to writing, printing, and now digital media.
